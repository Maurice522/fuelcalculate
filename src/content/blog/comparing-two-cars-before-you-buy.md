When comparing two cars before buying, purchase price gets most of the attention, but fuel cost is often the larger number over the years you'll actually own the vehicle. A car that costs less upfront but noticeably more to run can end up the more expensive choice within a few years — and the reverse is just as common.

## Why mileage claims aren't enough to compare on

Brochure or test-cycle mileage figures are useful for a rough sense of a vehicle's efficiency class, but they're measured under standardised conditions that don't match everyone's real driving. Two cars with similar claimed mileage can perform quite differently once you account for your actual mix of city and highway driving, traffic conditions, and typical load. Where possible, look for real-world mileage figures from owners with a similar driving pattern to yours, not just the official number.

## Comparing across fuel types, not just within one

The more useful comparison is often between fuel types, not just between two petrol cars. A diesel version of the same model, a CNG variant, or an EV alternative can have a meaningfully different running cost even if the purchase price is similar — and the "right" choice depends on how much you actually drive. High-distance buyers have more room for a fuel-efficient (or CNG/EV) option's advantage to pay off; low-distance buyers may find the running-cost gap too small to matter, making other factors more decisive.

## Don't forget maintenance and depreciation

Fuel cost is one part of ownership cost, not the whole thing. Diesel vehicles typically cost more to service. EVs have different maintenance profiles (no oil changes, but potential battery-related costs down the line). Resale value also varies by fuel type and can shift with market trends — a car that's cheap to run today isn't necessarily the one that holds its value best.

## A practical way to compare

Rather than relying on marketing claims or a single "which is better" opinion, build the comparison from your own numbers:

1. Get realistic mileage figures for each vehicle on the fuel type you're considering — from owner reports if you can, not just official ratings.
2. Use your actual typical distance (daily commute plus your realistic occasional longer trips), not a national average.
3. Compare the running cost difference against the purchase price difference to see how many years of driving it takes to break even.
