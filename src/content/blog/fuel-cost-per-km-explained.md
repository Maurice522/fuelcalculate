Ask most drivers what their car costs to run and you'll get a pump price, a mileage figure, or a rough monthly guess — rarely the one number that actually makes those comparable: cost per kilometre. It's the figure that turns two unrelated numbers (price and efficiency) into something you can actually compare across vehicles, fuels, and trips.

## Why price alone doesn't tell you anything useful

A lower price per litre doesn't mean a cheaper vehicle to run if it also has worse mileage — and a higher price per litre doesn't mean an expensive one if the mileage is high enough to compensate. Comparing fuel types or vehicles by price alone is comparing half an equation. Cost per km is what happens when you actually finish the calculation.

## The formula

For most fuels, it's straightforward: **cost per km = price per unit ÷ mileage**. For EVs, it's slightly different because efficiency is usually expressed as energy used per 100 km rather than distance per unit of energy: **cost per km = (tariff per kWh × efficiency in kWh/100km) ÷ 100**. Both versions answer the same underlying question — what does one kilometre of driving actually cost — just adapted to how each fuel type's efficiency is normally measured.

## What it lets you actually do

Once you have a real cost-per-km figure, several things become possible that aren't possible with price or mileage alone:

- **Compare fuel types fairly.** Petrol, diesel, CNG, LPG, E20 and EV all have different price structures and units — litres, kg, kWh. Cost per km puts them on the same footing.
- **Compare vehicles fairly.** Two cars with different mileage and possibly different fuel types become directly comparable once you're looking at cost per km instead of two separate numbers that don't relate to each other.
- **Scale to any distance instantly.** Once you know cost per km, any trip length is just a multiplication — no need to recalculate from scratch for a longer or shorter drive.

## Use your real numbers, not defaults

The accuracy of a cost-per-km figure depends entirely on the mileage and price you put into it. A claimed mileage figure or an outdated price will give you a technically-correct-looking number that doesn't match reality. Where possible, use your own observed mileage (litres filled ÷ distance driven) and your current local price.
