Most household budgets treat fuel as a rough guess — "about this much a month" — based on a vague sense of how often the tank gets filled. That works until it doesn't, usually when a month's actual spend comes in noticeably higher than expected. Building the number properly instead of guessing takes the same few minutes it takes to guess badly.

## Start by listing every regular trip, not just the main commute

A household fuel budget usually has more moving parts than one person's commute: a partner's separate commute, school runs, regular errands, weekend driving. Each of these has its own distance and frequency, and lumping them into a single vague "monthly fuel spend" estimate is exactly how the real number ends up surprising you. List them out separately, even roughly, before trying to total them.

## Use real driving days, not calendar days

A common budgeting mistake is multiplying a daily cost by 30. Most households don't drive every single day the same way — work-from-home days, weekends with different patterns, occasional days without the car at all. Using your actual typical driving days per month, not a flat 30, is a small adjustment that noticeably changes the total.

## Multiple vehicles need separate calculations

If your household has more than one vehicle, resist the temptation to average them into one estimate. Different vehicles have different mileage, and often different fuel types entirely — a shared "average" figure hides the fact that one vehicle might be costing far more per kilometre than the other. Calculate each separately, then add the totals.

## Revisit it when prices move

Fuel prices don't stay flat, and a budget calculated once at the start of the year can drift noticeably out of date within a few months, especially with larger price swings. A household fuel budget is worth treating as something to check in on periodically, not a number you set once and forget.
