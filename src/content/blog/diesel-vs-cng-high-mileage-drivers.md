For a high-mileage driver — long commutes, commercial use, or anyone regularly covering serious daily distance — the fuel-cost decision matters more than it does for occasional drivers, because small per-km differences get multiplied by a much bigger number. Diesel and CNG represent two very different bets for this kind of driving.

## Diesel's case: availability and highway efficiency

Diesel's advantage for high-mileage driving is largely about consistency: pumps are everywhere, refuelling is fast, and diesel engines tend to be at their most efficient on longer, steadier drives — exactly the kind of driving high-mileage users often do. The trade-off is that diesel doesn't offer the same price-per-unit advantage that CNG does, so the saving comes mostly from efficiency rather than a cheap fuel price.

## CNG's case: a real price advantage, with a real catch

CNG is usually priced meaningfully below diesel per unit, and for high-mileage use, that gap compounds fast. The catch is availability — CNG stations are far less widespread, and for a high-mileage driver, that's a bigger problem than it is for someone doing an occasional short trip. A cheaper fuel that requires regular detours or route planning around station locations carries a real hidden cost that a simple price comparison doesn't capture.

## The distance threshold question

There isn't a fixed distance above which one fuel "wins" — it depends on the actual price gap in your area, your vehicle's mileage on each fuel, and how reliably CNG stations sit along your normal routes. What's true in general is that the higher your distance, the more a small per-km difference matters, which is exactly why high-mileage drivers should be running their own numbers rather than relying on a general rule that might describe someone else's driving pattern.

## Vehicle and maintenance considerations

Beyond fuel cost, high-mileage use puts more wear on a vehicle regardless of fuel type, which makes maintenance cost a bigger factor in the overall decision than it would be for light use. Diesel engines and CNG kits both have their own maintenance profiles worth factoring in separately from the pure fuel-cost comparison — a fuel-cost win can be partly or fully offset by higher upkeep costs over high-mileage use.
