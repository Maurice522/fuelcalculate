Most people know roughly what they pay per litre at the pump, but very few know what their commute actually costs them over a month. That gap matters more than it seems — a daily trip that "feels cheap" one fill-up at a time can add up to a serious chunk of your monthly budget once you multiply it out properly.

## The formula, broken down

The core calculation is simple: **fuel used = distance ÷ mileage**, and **cost = fuel used × price per unit**. The part people usually get wrong isn't the formula — it's the inputs. Two mistakes show up constantly:

1. **Using claimed mileage instead of real mileage.** Manufacturer or test-cycle mileage figures are measured under ideal conditions. Real city commuting — traffic, AC, short trips that never let the engine reach an efficient operating temperature — routinely returns lower numbers. If you've never checked, reset your trip computer or do the math manually (litres filled ÷ distance covered since the last fill-up) for a couple of weeks before trusting any preset figure.
2. **Forgetting the return trip.** A "20 km commute" is often 40 km of actual driving once you count the way home. This single oversight can make your estimate half of what you're really spending.

## Turning a single trip into a monthly number

Once you have an accurate one-way cost, the monthly figure isn't just "trip cost × 30." You should account for:

- **Round trip or one-way** — most commutes are round trips, so double the distance before applying your per-km cost.
- **Actual driving days**, not calendar days. If you work from home two days a week or take public transport occasionally, using 30 days overstates your real cost.
- **Price drift.** Fuel prices move, sometimes significantly over a month. A number calculated once at the start of the month can be meaningfully off by the end of it.

## Why this is worth doing properly

The value of an accurate commute-cost figure isn't the number itself — it's what it lets you compare against. Once you know your real monthly fuel spend, you can meaningfully weigh it against public transport, carpooling, or a different vehicle, instead of guessing. A "small" daily cost that looks trivial per trip often turns out to be one of the larger recurring expenses in a household budget once it's actually added up.
