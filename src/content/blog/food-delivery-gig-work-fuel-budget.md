Delivery work — food, groceries, courier — has a fuel cost pattern that doesn't look like a normal commute or even a rideshare shift. It's made up of many short trips, frequent stops, and a lot of low-speed or idling time, all of which affect fuel use in ways a single "distance × mileage" estimate tends to understate.

## Short trips are less efficient than they look

A vehicle's efficiency is generally worse over short distances than longer, steadier ones — the engine spends a larger share of a short trip working from cold or from a stop, which is the least efficient part of any drive. Delivery work is almost entirely short trips by nature, which means a mileage figure based on longer average driving (a manufacturer figure, or your own observed mileage from a different kind of use) will usually overstate how efficient you're actually being during a delivery shift.

## Idle time adds up

Waiting outside a restaurant or a customer's building with the engine running is common in delivery work, and it's fuel cost that doesn't correspond to any distance travelled. Over a full shift, this can be a meaningful share of total fuel used — often more than people expect, precisely because it doesn't feel like part of the "driving."

## Two-wheelers vs cars for delivery

Two-wheelers dominate food delivery for good reason: far better fuel efficiency for short, stop-start trips, easier parking, and lower running cost overall. If you're deciding between a bike and a car for delivery work, the fuel-cost gap is usually large enough to be one of the more important factors in that decision, on top of the practical advantages.

## Working out your real per-shift cost

Because delivery driving doesn't match "average" driving conditions, the most reliable number comes from your own tracked data rather than a generic mileage figure: total distance covered in a shift (your delivery app usually shows this) divided into how much fuel you actually used. Doing this over a few shifts gives you a realistic mileage figure specific to delivery conditions, rather than borrowing a number that describes different driving entirely.
