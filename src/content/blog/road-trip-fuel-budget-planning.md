Estimating fuel cost for a daily commute is straightforward once you know your numbers. A road trip is different — longer distance means small errors compound, and there are a few extra variables that don't matter for a short commute but genuinely move the number on a long drive.

## Start with the real distance, not the straight-line one

Route distance from a map is your starting point, but road trips rarely go exactly to plan. Detours, fuel stops that require leaving the highway, and the return leg all add real kilometres. Build in a buffer — even 10-15% — rather than budgeting against the theoretical shortest route.

## Highway mileage isn't your city mileage

This is the single biggest source of road-trip fuel estimate errors: using a mileage figure you've observed in city driving for a trip that's mostly highway, or vice versa. Highway driving at a steady speed is usually more efficient than stop-start city traffic, but higher speeds past a certain point start working against you too. If you have highway-specific mileage data for your vehicle, use it. If you only have a city-driving figure, treat your estimate as conservative rather than precise.

## Prices vary along the route

Fuel prices aren't the same everywhere, especially crossing state or regional lines — a price gap that seems trivial per litre adds up over a full tank, multiplied by however many fill-ups a long trip needs. If you know roughly which cities or regions you'll be passing through, checking [prices by city](/cities) for a couple of key stops gives you a more realistic average than assuming your home city's price applies for the whole trip.

## Account for load and passengers

A fully loaded car — luggage, extra passengers, a roof box — genuinely uses more fuel than the same car driven solo and empty. It's not usually a huge effect, but on a long trip it's enough to matter, and it's the kind of thing a quick mental estimate tends to ignore entirely.
